PennHealthX Course · Fall 2026

From clinical need to pitch night: a hands-on course in healthcare innovation and entrepreneurship.

Over twelve weekly sessions, you and your team will identify an unmet clinical need, validate it against the market, prototype a solution, and learn how patents, FDA regulation, funding, and reimbursement shape what gets built. Guest experts from venture capital, technology transfer, and medical device translation join along the way. The course culminates in a final Pitch Night in December.

How the course works

Three phases, one venture

Each session pairs a focused lecture with an interactive component. You will form a team in Week 1 and carry a single clinical need through every phase of the course, ending with a pitch to a live audience.

Weeks 1 – 3

Discover

Define an unmet clinical need, analyze it from every stakeholder's perspective, size up the competitive landscape, and apply human-centered design and lean startup principles to build a minimum viable product.

Weeks 4 – 6

Protect & Navigate

Learn what makes an invention patentable and how technology transfer works in academic medicine, build a pitch deck that tells a compelling story, and map your product onto the right FDA regulatory pathway.

Weeks 7 – 12

Launch

Hear from physician entrepreneurs, plan funding and clinical validation, estimate market size and reimbursement models, refine your pitch in two weeks of office hours, and present at Pitch Night.

Schedule

Twelve weeks, start to finish

Sessions meet Mondays at 5:00 PM ET unless noted. Expand a week to see its learning objectives and interactive component.

  1. Mon, Sep 215:00 PM ET
    Week 1

    Clinical Needs Finding and Assessment

    Learning objectives

    1. Define an unmet clinical need in healthcare.
    2. Analyze a problem from the different perspectives of key healthcare stakeholders.
    3. Apply root-cause analysis to identify the underlying causes of a clinical problem.

    Interactive component

    Students will identify at least one unmet clinical need and develop a solution-neutral clinical needs statement. This activity also kicks off team formation for the final course project.

  2. Mon, Sep 285:00 PM ET
    Week 2

    Validating the Problem & Competitive Landscape

    Learning objectives

    1. Describe the difference between clinical significance and market significance.
    2. Analyze existing solutions for a clinical need from both direct and indirect competitors.
    3. Recognize gaps in the market and potential opportunities for differentiation.

    Interactive component

    Students will identify existing solutions, potential competitors, and gaps in the competitive landscape for their identified clinical need.

  3. Mon, Oct 55:00 PM ET
    Week 3

    Ideation, Prototyping, and MVP Development

    Learning objectives

    1. Apply human-centered design principles to develop solutions around the needs and experiences of end users.
    2. Explain lean startup principles and the importance of testing assumptions early.
    3. Differentiate a minimum viable product (MVP) from a fully developed product.
    4. Identify the key assumptions and risks underlying a proposed innovation.
  4. Mon, Oct 125:00 PM ET
    Week 4 Guest speaker · Michael Hartman

    Intellectual Property and Patenting Technology

    Learning objectives

    1. Explain the role of intellectual property in building and commercializing medical innovations.
    2. Identify the characteristics of a patentable invention, including the concepts of novelty, non-obviousness, and utility.
    3. Describe the patenting process, including provisional and non-provisional patent applications, the role of the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO), and key considerations for pursuing patent protection.
    4. Recognize common intellectual property considerations in academic medicine, including technology transfer, university ownership, and invention disclosure.

    Interactive component

    Students will work in small groups to evaluate the patentability of one or more hypothetical medical innovations (e.g., a medical device, digital health application, or diagnostic tool) in a case study. Each group will discuss:

    • Is this innovation likely patentable? Why or why not?
    • What aspects of the invention would you seek to protect?
    • What would be the next steps in the patenting process?
    • What potential intellectual property or commercialization challenges should the inventors consider?

    Meet Michael Hartman →

  5. Mon, Oct 195:00 PM ET
    Week 5

    How to Create a Pitch Deck

    Learning objectives

    1. Identify the elements of an effective pitch deck, including the unmet need, product, market opportunity and competitive landscape, business model, and funding needs.
    2. Develop a narrative connecting the unmet clinical need, proposed solution, and value proposition.
    3. Adapt a pitch deck and articulate funding needs to different target audiences.

    Interactive component

    Students will work in small groups to create and present a short pitch deck and presentation for a hypothetical medical innovation.

  6. Tue, Oct 2712:00 PM ET
    Week 6 Guest speaker · Katherine Reuther

    Navigating the FDA Regulatory Pathway

    Learning objectives

    1. Describe the FDA's role in regulating medical technologies and enabling safe translation of innovations to patients.
    2. Classify medical technologies by risk level (Class I, II, III) and explain how risk classification influences regulatory requirements.
    3. Differentiate between the major FDA regulatory pathways (510(k), De Novo, and PMA) and recognize how these pathways relate to device risk and intended use.
    4. Recognize how regulatory strategy shapes product development, including evidence generation, testing requirements, timelines, and post-market surveillance.

    Interactive component

    Students will work in small groups to evaluate a hypothetical medical innovation and determine its likely FDA regulatory pathway. Each group will discuss:

    • What is the intended use of this technology?
    • What is its risk class (I, II, or III)?
    • Which pathway is most appropriate (510(k), De Novo, or PMA) and why?
    • What type of testing or evidence would likely be needed?

    Meet Katherine Reuther →

  7. Mon, Nov 25:00 PM ET
    Week 7 Guest speakers

    Physician Entrepreneurship Panel

    Learning objectives

    1. Describe pathways for physicians to pursue entrepreneurship and innovation.
    2. Explain the unique role of physicians in identifying and solving healthcare problems.
    3. Recognize common challenges, risks, and lessons learned from physician entrepreneurs.
    4. Identify opportunities and next steps for exploring entrepreneurship during medical school.
  8. Mon, Nov 95:00 PM ET
    Week 8 Guest speaker

    Funding, Clinical Validation, and Commercialization

    Learning objectives

    1. Identify major sources of funding for healthcare startups, including grants, venture capital, angel investors, accelerators, and institutional resources.
    2. Explain the role of clinical validation and evidence generation in demonstrating that a healthcare innovation is safe, effective, and clinically meaningful.
    3. Describe key milestones used to build traction and advance a healthcare innovation toward commercialization.
    4. Explain how translational research can move an innovation from academic discovery to clinical application and commercialization.
  9. Mon, Nov 165:00 PM ET
    Week 9 Guest speaker · Sherman Leung

    Market Opportunity, Reimbursement, and Scalability

    Learning objectives

    1. Craft a value proposition based on the target consumer and unmet clinical needs.
    2. Estimate market size using different frameworks, including top-down vs. bottom-up market sizing and estimation of total addressable market (TAM), serviceable addressable market (SAM), and serviceable obtainable market (SOM).
    3. Describe the types of payers in healthcare and reimbursement models, including fee-for-service, bundled payments, subscription/SaaS payments, licensing, and direct-to-consumer.
    4. Analyze factors that impact the scalability of a healthcare product or business.

    Interactive component

    Students will work in small groups to evaluate the market size and potential reimbursement models for either a hypothetical medical innovation or a case study of an existing technology (e.g., a medical device, digital health application, or diagnostic tool). Each group will discuss:

    • What is the target customer for the product?
    • What are the TAM, SAM, and SOM for the product?
    • What models could be used for product reimbursement?
    • What factors might be facilitators or barriers to market growth?

Guest speakers

Learn from people who have done it

Three sessions are led by experts who work on intellectual property, FDA regulation, and go-to-market strategy every day.

Michael Hartman, Esq.

Associate General Counsel, Office of General Counsel, University of Pennsylvania

Week 4 · Intellectual Property and Patenting Technology

Michael Hartman is Associate General Counsel in the University of Pennsylvania's Office of General Counsel, where he represents Penn Medicine on innovation, technology transfer, intellectual property, and corporate and operational matters. He joined the office in 2023 after serving as Associate Director of Corporate Contracts at the Penn Center for Innovation (PCI), the University's technology transfer office responsible for patenting, licensing, and commercializing Penn discoveries. He earned his undergraduate and law degrees from Duquesne University.

Katherine Reuther, PhD, MBA

Executive Director, Penn Health-Tech; Practice Associate Professor of Bioengineering, University of Pennsylvania

Week 6 · Navigating the FDA Regulatory Pathway

Katherine Reuther leads Penn Health-Tech, the University of Pennsylvania's center for advancing new medical devices and health technologies, and is a Practice Associate Professor in Bioengineering. She previously served as Senior Lecturer in Design, Innovation, and Entrepreneurship in Columbia University's Department of Biomedical Engineering, where she helped create and led the Columbia Biomedical Engineering Technology Accelerator (BiomedX), guiding more than 60 technologies to $80M in follow-on funding and 18 licenses. She has managed NIH translational programs including C3i and RADx, is a nationally trained NIH I-Corps instructor, co-founded the orthopedic device start-up Syntegrity Biomedical, and received Columbia's Presidential Award for Outstanding Teaching in 2021. She holds a PhD in Bioengineering from Penn and an Executive MBA from Columbia.

Sherman Leung, MD

Emergency Medicine Resident, Stanford; Venture Fellow, a16z Bio + Health

Week 9 · Market Opportunity, Reimbursement, and Scalability

Sherman Leung is an emergency medicine resident physician at Stanford and a venture fellow at Andreessen Horowitz (a16z) Bio + Health, where he focuses on AI in healthcare, new care delivery models, and Medicaid and payer infrastructure. As a medical student at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, he helped launch AlleyCorp Healthcare, a physician-led venture capital firm and venture studio, leading investments in tech-enabled specialty care and value-based care companies. He was an early product manager at PatientPing, named to MedTech Boston's "40 Under 40 Healthcare Innovators," and later a clinical product leader at Pearl Health and Counsel Health. He founded MD+, a nonprofit supporting more than 4,000 aspiring physician-innovators, and holds a BS in Computer Science and an MS in Management Science & Engineering from Stanford.
